Appliance Care in Desert Heat: St. George Summers Are Hard on Fridges and Dryers

St. George summers are genuinely hard on household appliances. Weeks of triple-digit heat, fine red dust that finds its way into everything, and dry desert air strain the two appliances that work by moving heat: your refrigerator and your dryer. A few simple habits, done a couple of times a year, can prevent the most common heat-season breakdowns.

Why Desert Heat Stresses Appliances

A refrigerator does not create cold; it moves heat from inside the cabinet to the room around it. When the room is hot and the coils are dusty, that job gets harder, the compressor runs longer, and wear adds up. Dryers face the same problem in reverse: they must push hot, moist air out through a vent, and lint buildup plus hot outdoor air makes the system work against resistance. Add St. George's dust — coating coils, clogging screens, settling into vents — and appliances here need more attention than the same machines in a mild climate.

Refrigerator Care for Hot Weather

Clean the condenser coils. This is the single most valuable habit for a desert refrigerator. Dust and pet hair insulate the coils and trap heat. Unplug the fridge, vacuum the coils with a brush attachment (behind the rear panel or under the front grille), and do it at least twice a year — more often if you have shedding pets. Late spring, before the heat arrives, is the ideal time.

Give it room to breathe. A refrigerator wedged tight into its opening cannot release heat. Leave some clearance around the sides and top, and keep boxes and bags from piling up against the back.

Check the door seals. Heat sneaks in through a tired gasket all summer long. Clean the seal with warm water and mild soap to remove grit, and test it with the dollar-bill method: if the bill slides out of the closed door with no resistance, the seal is weak in that spot.

Set temperatures once, correctly. Around 37–38°F for the refrigerator and 0°F for the freezer. Turning the dial colder does not cool food faster — it only makes the compressor run longer.

Be thoughtful about garage refrigerators. A second fridge in an uninsulated garage works brutally hard in July and August, and older units may struggle to keep food at safe temperatures. Clean coils and good airflow help, and a reasonably full fridge holds cold better than an empty one.

Dryer Care for Hot Weather

Keep the vent path clear — it matters even more in summer. Your dryer pushes hot, moist air out through the vent. A restriction makes dry times longer, runs the machine hotter, and trapped lint is a fire hazard. Clean the lint screen every load, wash it occasionally to remove softener residue, and check that the outside vent opens freely and blows strong when the dryer runs.

Inspect the vent hose. Look behind the dryer for crushing or kinks, especially if the machine was recently moved. Short, smooth, and straight is the goal.

Avoid the hottest hours for heavy laundry when you can. The dryer adds heat and humidity to the house while it runs. Early morning loads are easier on the house — and the dryer does not have to fight hot intake air.

Watch for the warning signs. Clothes taking noticeably longer to dry is nearly always the first symptom of a venting problem or a part beginning to fail. Do not shrug it off — the machine is already working harder than it should.

A Simple Twice-a-Year Routine

For most St. George homes — including our neighbors in Washington, Hurricane, Santa Clara, Ivins, and over the state line in Mesquite — this short routine in spring and fall covers the essentials:

  1. Vacuum refrigerator coils and check door seals
  2. Clean the dryer lint screen thoroughly and check outdoor vent airflow
  3. Look behind both appliances for dust buildup, kinks, or anything stored where it blocks air
  4. Wipe the dishwasher filter and check under the sink for slow leaks while you are at it

None of this requires opening an appliance cabinet or touching sealed components, wiring, or refrigerant — and it should not. Those systems are for trained technicians.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my refrigerator run all the time in the summer? Longer run times are normal when kitchen temperatures climb, because the fridge has more heat to move. Nonstop running combined with warmer-than-usual food, though, usually means dirty coils, a weak door seal, or a developing problem worth checking.

Does dust really damage appliances? Dust does not usually break a part directly — it insulates coils, clogs vents and screens, and makes motors and compressors run hotter and longer. That extra strain is what shortens appliance life in desert homes.

My dryer vent blows outside. Do I still need to check it? Yes. The most common restrictions happen in the transition hose behind the dryer and at the exterior hood, where lint mats together. Strong airflow at the outside vent while the dryer runs is the test.
